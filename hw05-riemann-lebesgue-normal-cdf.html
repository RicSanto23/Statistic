Summary

Riemann sums cut the domain into n pieces and sample f; Lebesgue sums cut the range into k levels and weigh each level by the measure of its preimage. On every Riemann-integrable function both converge to the same number. For sin x the fitted error slopes are … for the Riemann midpoint rule and … for the Lebesgue lower sum: the midpoint rule is faster on smooth functions, but the Lebesgue sum is a guaranteed bracket, needs only that f is measurable, and gives the right answer on the Dirichlet function (0) and on the indicator of a fat Cantor set (1/2), which is not Riemann integrable at all, where Riemann sums can return 0 or 1 depending on the sample points.

For Φ(x), no elementary formula exists (Liouville), so the page tests seven methods against 12 published reference values and against an independent 70-digit BigInt computation. A hybrid of a positive-term power series (for |x| < 2) and a continued fraction for the tail (otherwise) matches the references to a relative error of at most …, including at x = −10 where Φ ≈ 7.6 × 10⁻²⁴.

What surprised me most: the “obvious” methods fail in different places. The alternating erf series already has a 4% relative error at x = −6 and returns garbage at −8, and any formula that returns 1 − Φ(−x) or ½ + ∫ loses the whole tail to cancellation, even though it looks accurate in absolute terms.

Two ways to cut the area

Riemann. Cut [a, b] into n pieces of width h = (b − a)/n, pick a point xi in each (left end, midpoint, right end) and add f(xi)·h. Lebesgue. Cut the range [0, H] into k levels yi = iH/k and add yi · μ(f−1(yi, yi+1]). Using yi (the lower value of each level) gives a lower sum, using yi+1 an upper sum; the mid-level sum is their average.

The Lebesgue sum needs the measure of a level set. Estimating it by sampling the domain would just reproduce a Riemann sum, so I compute m(t) = μ{f > t} from the function itself: exactly for six functions (for example m(t) = 1 − √t for x²), by bisection for sin x/x (monotone on [0, π]), and by a measure-theory argument for Dirichlet and Thomae. The level set is then m(yi) − m(yi+1). For the unbounded 1/√x the range is cut at Y = √k and the tail {f > Y} is added with its lower value Y.

Riemann: Σi f(xi)·h    Lebesgue: Σi yi·[ m(yi) − m(yi+1) ]    and  ∫ f = ∫0∞ m(t) dt

Where the two approaches behave differently

    

      
Smooth functions. Left and right sums converge like 1/n (unless the endpoint values cancel: for sin x on [0, π] they are trapezoid sums and are second order), the midpoint rule like 1/n². The Lebesgue lower and upper sums bracket the integral and are off by at most H/k times the measure of the domain, so they converge like 1/k whatever the function; the mid-level sum is usually better. Riemann sums win in raw accuracy per evaluation when f is smooth, and they need no preimage measure, which for a general f is the hard part.

      
Jump discontinuities. Both are first-order, but the midpoint rule can be exact by luck (when no midpoint falls on the jump), as in the two step functions with an even n. This is an accident of the grid, not a property of the method.

      
Unbounded 1/√x. The Riemann left sum is infinite (f(0) = ∞); midpoint and right sums converge slowly, like n−1/2. The Lebesgue lower sum increases to 2 from below, which is exactly how the Lebesgue integral of a non-negative function is defined (a supremum of simple functions).

      
Dirichlet (1 on rationals, 0 elsewhere). Every upper Riemann sum is 1 and every lower sum is 0, so the Riemann integral does not exist; sums with rational tags (all grid points are rational) give 1, with irrational tags 0. The rationals have measure 0, so every Lebesgue sum is exactly 0 and ∫ f = 0. Thomae (1/q at p/q): continuous at every irrational and discontinuous at every rational, so by Lebesgue’s criterion it is Riemann integrable, with integral 0. Riemann sums converge to 0 slowly, because the grid points have small denominators; the Lebesgue sum is 0 at once, since {f > t} is a finite set.

      
Fat Cantor set C (remove from [0, 1] an open middle interval of length 4−j from each remaining piece at stage j). It is closed and nowhere dense, yet its measure is 1 − Σ 2j−14−j = ½. The indicator of C is discontinuous at every point of C, a set of positive measure, so it is not Riemann integrable (Lebesgue’s criterion): every upper Darboux sum is 1 and every lower sum is 0. Riemann sums on a regular grid happen to approach ½ slowly (the grid behaves like a random sample of the set), but nothing guarantees it: tags chosen inside C give 1, tags chosen in the removed gaps give 0. The Lebesgue integral is simply μ(C) = ½, whatever the tags. Unlike Dirichlet, this function can be evaluated numerically, so it also shows the failure of Riemann sums on a function you can actually compute.

Why there is no elementary antiderivative

    
Φ(x) = ∫−∞x φ(t) dt = ½ + (1/√(2π)) ∫0x e−t²/2 dt    =  ½ [ 1 + erf(x/√2) ]

    

      
Φ would be elementary only if e−x²/2 had an elementary antiderivative, because Φ′ = φ. Liouville’s theorem (1830s; made systematic by Risch and Rosenlicht) gives an exact test for integrands of the form f eg, and the Gaussian fails it:

    

    

      
L1
∫ f eg dx is elementary  ⇔  there is a rational h with h′ + g′h = f,  and then ∫ f eg dx = h eg
Liouville’s criterion, for rational functions f and g with g not constant.

      
L2
f = 1,  g = −x²/2  ⇒  we need a rational h with h′ − x·h = 1
The constant 1/√(2π) in φ does not change anything. If h = 0 the left side is 0, so h ≠ 0.

      
L3
h a polynomial of degree d ≥ 0  ⇒  deg(h′ − x·h) = d + 1 ≥ 1
x·h has degree d + 1 while h′ has degree below d, so nothing cancels the leading term: the left side is never the constant 1.

      
L4
h with a pole of order k ≥ 1 at a point c  ⇒  h′ has a pole of order k + 1 at c,  x·h of order at most k
So h′ − x·h still has a pole at c and cannot equal 1. Every rational function is either a polynomial or has a pole, so L3 and L4 cover all cases.

      
L5
no rational solution  ⇒  ∫ e−x²/2 dx is not elementary  ⇒  Φ is not elementary
By L1. An elementary Φ would be an elementary antiderivative of φ.

    


    
What erf adds, and why numbers still need an algorithm

    

      
What erf changes. The error function erf(z) = (2/√π) ∫0z e−t² dt is a name for exactly that non-elementary integral. With it Φ has an exact closed form, Φ(x) = ½ erfc(−x/√2), and erf has clean properties (odd, a known Taylor series, a continued fraction for the tail). But a name is not a number: to get a value you still need an algorithm, such as quadrature, a series, a continued fraction or a fitted rational approximation, which is what libraries do.

      
Two clarifications. Liouville’s theorem is about antiderivatives; the definite integral over the whole line is perfectly computable, ∫−∞∞ e−x²/2 dx = √(2π) (square the integral and switch to polar coordinates), which is why φ integrates to 1 even though Φ(x) has no closed form. And erf is the regularized incomplete gamma function, erf(z) = P(½, z²), so Φ belongs to the same family as the chi-square distribution functions.

    


    
Seven methods, and the code of the best one

    

      
The seven methods: trapezoid and Simpson on ½ + ∫0x φ with n pieces; the Maclaurin series of erf, (2/√π) Σ (−1)j z2j+1 / (j!(2j+1)) with z = x/√2; the Kummer series ½ + φ(x) Σ x2j+1/(1·3·5···(2j+1)), which has only positive terms; Abramowitz–Stegun 7.1.26, a five-term fit of erf with error up to 1.5 × 10⁻⁷; Abramowitz–Stegun 26.2.17, a five-term fit of Φ directly (error up to 7.5 × 10⁻⁸); and a hybrid: the Kummer series for |x| < 2, and for the tail the continued fraction Φ(−x) = φ(x) / (x + 1/(x + 2/(x + 3/(x + …)))) evaluated backwards with 80 terms, so the small tail is computed directly, never as 1 − Φ.

The hybrid uses the series for |x| < 2 and the continued fraction beyond. The table below (relative errors against the 70-digit reference) shows why: the series error grows as Φ(−x) shrinks (an absolute error of about 1e-16 divided by a smaller and smaller Φ), while the continued fraction needs more terms when x is small. The threshold at 2 is where the two errors cross: there the series (about 5e-15) and an 80-term fraction (about 9e-15) are about equally accurate. Below 2 the series is better; above 2 the fraction is better, and the series loses digits (about 9e-14 at x = −3, 6e-10 at x = −5). More terms would let the fraction take over at a smaller |x|.
