Exercise 1: Welford's recurrence formulas

Knuth's recurrence for the mean

After n scores x1, …, xn the sample mean is Mn = (x1 + … + xn)/n. Knuth's approach is to split off the newest score and write everything else with the previous mean, so that the old scores never have to be visited again.

M1
n·Mn = x1 + … + xn−1 + xn = (n−1)·Mn−1 + xn

The first n−1 scores add up to n−1 times their own mean.

M2
Mn = Mn−1 + (xn − Mn−1)/n

Divide by n and use (n−1)·Mn−1 = n·Mn−1 − Mn−1. This is Knuth's recurrence; the start is M0 = 0.

Give the new score a name, δn = xn − Mn−1, the surprise: how far the new score lands from the mean so far. The mean moves a fraction 1/n of the way towards it. Three small facts follow, and the next derivation uses all of them:

(a)  Mn − Mn−1 = δn/n
(b)  xn − Mn = δn − δn/n = δn·(n−1)/n
(c)  (x1 − Mn−1) + … + (xn−1 − Mn−1) = 0  (deviations from their own mean add up to zero)

Welford's recurrence for the sum of squares

The sum of squares is SSn = (x1 − Mn)² + … + (xn − Mn)². The approach is the same: split off xn, and rewrite the rest in terms of the previous quantity, here SSn−1. The difficulty is that every deviation in the first n−1 terms is now measured from the new mean Mn, not from Mn−1.

S1
SSn = Σi<n (xi − Mn)² + (xn − Mn)²

Split off the newest score.

S2
Σi<n (xi − Mn)² = Σi<n (xi − Mn−1)² − 2(Mn − Mn−1)·Σi<n (xi − Mn−1) + (n−1)(Mn − Mn−1)²

Write xi − Mn = (xi − Mn−1) − (Mn − Mn−1) and expand the square.

S3
Σi<n (xi − Mn)² = SSn−1 + 0 + (n−1)·(δn/n)²

The first sum is SSn−1. The middle sum is zero by fact (c), so the cross term disappears. The last term uses fact (a).

S4
(xn − Mn)² = (δn·(n−1)/n)²

Fact (b).

S5
SSn = SSn−1 + δn²·[(n−1)/n² + (n−1)²/n²] = SSn−1 + δn²·(n−1)/n

Add S3 and S4. The bracket is (n−1)(1 + n−1)/n² = (n−1)/n.

S6
SSn = SSn−1 + (xn − Mn−1)·(xn − Mn)

By fact (b), δn²·(n−1)/n = δn·(xn − Mn). The start is SS0 = 0. With the first score, δ1 = x1 and x1 − M1 = 0, so SS1 = 0, as it should be.

Both factors in the last line are already at hand: δn was needed for the mean, and xn − Mn uses the mean that was just updated. In exact arithmetic the increment is never negative, because the two factors always have the same sign (fact b). So SS can only grow, and the variance can never come out negative.

The recurrence for the variance

The sample variance divides SS by n−1: sn² = SSn/(n−1) for n ≥ 2. Divide Welford's recurrence by n−1 and use SSn−1 = (n−2)·sn−1²:

V1
sn² = SSn−1/(n−1) + δn²/n = ((n−2)/(n−1))·sn−1² + δn²/n   (n ≥ 2)

The start is s2² = δ2²/2 = (x2 − x1)²/2.

V2
Vn = SSn/n = Vn−1 + [ δn·(xn − Mn) − Vn−1 ] / n

The same for the variance with divisor n (the plain average of squared deviations): multiply SSn = SSn−1 + δn(xn − Mn) by 1/n. This has exactly Knuth's shape, new = old + (input − old)/n. So Vn is the running mean of the numbers δi·(xi − Mi), the way Mn is the running mean of the xi.

Mn = Mn−1 + δn / n
SSn = SSn−1 + δn·(xn − Mn)
sn² = SSn / (n−1) = ((n−2)/(n−1))·sn−1² + δn² / n
with δn = xn − Mn−1, M0 = 0, SS0 = 0

To be sure, the recurrence is compared with the two-pass formula on 100,000 random numbers between 0 and 10. It is checked at several points on the way (after 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000 and 100,000 numbers), and the table reports the largest relative difference.

    

    

    


    
Why not the textbook one-pass formula?

    

      
The textbook shortcut keeps two running totals and computes SS = Σx² − (Σx)²/n at the end. It is also one pass, but it subtracts two huge, nearly equal numbers whenever the scores are far from zero, and the difference is lost in rounding. Welford's update only ever combines small numbers: the surprise δ is small even when the scores are large.

      
To see it, I add the same large offset to 10,000 scores (Binomial(10, 0.3), as in exercise 2). The variance does not depend on the offset, so the exact answer is the same on every row. The error columns are |value − exact| / exact.

My choice is Binomial(10, 0.3): think of ten independent defences on a system, each broken with probability 0.3, and the score is the number broken. Why the n−1. Write the deviations from the true mean: SSn = Σ(xi − μ)² − n·(Mn − μ)². The first sum has expected value nσ². The second term has expected value n·σ²/n = σ². So E[SSn] = (n−1)σ²: the deviations from the sample's own mean are smaller than the deviations from the true mean, because the sample mean was chosen to fit the same scores. Dividing by n−1 repairs this exactly; dividing by n leaves a factor (n−1)/n.

    


    
Why this matters in inferential statistics

    

      
In practice nobody knows μ and σ². A hacker, or an analyst watching the attacks, has one trajectory of n scores and wants to learn about the distribution behind it. That is inference, and the comparison above is what makes it possible:

      
1. The sample mean and the sample variance are unbiased estimators. Averaged over many samples they hit μ and σ², so using them as estimates does not push the answer in one direction. The n−1 divisor is what keeps the variance honest.

      
2. They are consistent. The spread between hackers shrinks as n grows (see the next part), so with enough systems one trajectory is enough.

      
3. The variance is what measures the uncertainty of the mean. The sample mean has variance σ²/n, so its typical error is σ/√n. σ is unknown, but s is a good stand-in, which gives the confidence interval x̄ ± t · s/√n for μ, where t is the critical value of the t distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom. The table checks this on the hackers: how often does each hacker's own 95% interval contain the true μ? The plain version with 1.96 in place of t is shown next to it.

      
4. Welford makes it work on a stream. After every system the hacker has the mean, the variance and the interval, from three stored numbers, with no second pass and no rounding disaster.

Increasing n: how the variability changes

    

      
The averages above say nothing about how far one hacker is from the truth. For that I look at the spread between hackers: the standard deviation of the m sample means and of the m sample variances, for each n. Theory says the sample means have standard deviation σ/√n. For the sample variance the exact value is √Var(s²) with Var(s²) = μ4/n − σ⁴·(n−3)/(n(n−1)), where μ4 is the fourth central moment of the scores; for large n this is about √((μ4 − σ⁴)/n).

There are two knobs. The number of systems per hacker, n, decides how good each hacker's own estimate is. The number of hackers, m, decides how well the average over hackers is pinned down. Bonus: merging the hackers' streams

      
Welford's three numbers can be merged: the states of two hackers combine into the state of both of them together, without reading a single score again.

      

        The merge formulaCheck on the same hackersWithin and between
      

    

  

  

    
The merge formula

    

      
Take two groups of scores, A with state (nA, MA, SSA) and B with (nB, MB, SSB). Let n = nA + nB and δ = MB − MA. The combined mean sits between the two: M = MA + δ·nB/n, so M − MA = δ·nB/n and M − MB = −δ·nA/n. For the sum of squares, expand each group's deviations around its own mean exactly as in step S2 of exercise 1 (the cross terms vanish again):

    

    

      SS = [ SSA + nA·(δ·nB/n)² ] + [ SSB + nB·(δ·nA/n)² ] = SSA + SSB + δ²·nA·nB/n
    

    

      
With a group B of one score (nB = 1, SSB = 0) this is Welford's update again, so the update is the special case of the merge. The merge is what lets many machines each read their own part of the data and then combine the results, which is the natural way to process m hackers in parallel.

Within and between hackers

    

      
Merging all m states also shows where the total variation of the scores comes from. The total sum of squares splits exactly into what happens within each hacker's trajectory and what differs between the hackers' means: SStotal = Σ SSk + n·Σ(Mk − M)² (the sum over the m hackers, with n scores each). This identity is the merge formula applied again and again.

      
Both parts estimate σ². The within part gives ΣSSk / (m(n−1)), which is exactly the average of the m sample variances from the table above. The between part gives n·Σ(Mk − M)²/(m−1), which is n times the variance of the sample means. That is the other way to read σ²/n, the variance of the sample mean: multiplied back by n it must give σ². If the hackers differed in skill, the between part would come out larger than the within part.
