Summary

This page models m attackers who each attack the same n systems. The score on system i is a random step Xᵢ: either a breach or nothing (Bernoulli, P(Xᵢ = 1) = p), or a gain or a loss of ground (Rademacher, ±1 with probability ½ each). Each attacker is one sample trajectory, so m attackers give m independent samples of the total Sₙ, the average Sₙ/n and the normalized total Sₙ/√n. I simulate the attackers when the page opens, and compare every measured distribution, probability and variance with the exact theory. One experiment in section 3 is repeated with fewer and more attackers, to show what a suitable m is.

The three quantities behave in three different ways. The spread of the total grows like √n, the spread of the average shrinks like 1/√n, and the spread of Sₙ/√n stays fixed: the fitted variance slopes on a log-log plot are …, against the theoretical 1, −1 and 0. The average converges in probability: the share of attackers whose average misses the mean by more than 0.05 falls from … at … systems to … at …. In the Rademacher case Sₙ/√n approaches N(0, 1): the exact distance from the normal curve shrinks like …/√n, just under the Berry–Esseen bound 0.47/√n. For the interpretation, a bigger system makes the total score larger and more spread out, but the breach rate becomes predictable. More attackers do not change any single attacker's distribution; they add up the total damage, and they push the best attacker further ahead (… standard deviations above the mean among … attackers).

What surprised me most: after about … systems, the simulation with … attackers could no longer see the central limit theorem improving. The gap between Sₙ/√n and the normal curve keeps shrinking, but it falls below the random error that comes from having only m attackers, which is about 1/√m. To see the gap at n systems takes roughly …n attackers, so the number of attackers, not the number of systems, limits what the experiment can show.

The model: n systems, m attackers, i.i.d. increments

The penetration process is a sum of independent and identically distributed steps. One attacker going through the n systems is one sample trajectory, and m attackers are m independent trajectories.

Attacker j (from 1 to m) attacks systems 1, 2, …, n in order. On system i it gets the penetration score Xij. Its running score after k systems is Sk = X1 + … + Xk, so the path S₁, S₂, …, Sₙ is the attacker's trajectory. The steps are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.): every system is equally hard, attackers do not learn from one system to the next, and attackers do not help each other. Section 7 comes back to what happens when these assumptions fail.

Bernoulli model

Xi ∈ {0, 1},  P(Xi = 1) = p

Each system is either breached (1) or not (0). Sₙ counts the breached systems and has the binomial distribution Bin(n, p). Sₙ/n is the attacker's breach rate.

Mean μ = p, variance σ² = p(1 − p).

Rademacher model

Xi ∈ {−1, +1},  P(+1) = P(−1) = ½

Each system moves the attacker one step forward (a foothold gained) or one step back (detected and pushed out). Sₙ is a simple symmetric random walk: the net progress. It is the zero-mean, symmetric case.

Mean μ = 0, variance σ² = 1. Also X = 2B − 1 with B Bernoulli(½), so Sₙ = 2·Bin(n, ½) − n.

Because the steps are independent, means and variances add up. That gives the exact first two moments of the three quantities the assignment asks about, for any n:

E[Sn] = nμ,  Var(Sn) = nσ²
E[Sn/n] = μ,  Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n
E[Sn/√n] = √n μ,  Var(Sn/√n) = σ²

Read from left to right, the three charts answer one question in three ways: how far apart do the attackers end up? In the totals the paths fan out without limit, because the uncertainty about a sum of k steps grows like √k. Dividing by k squeezes them together, because √k/k = 1/√k → 0. Dividing by √k exactly cancels the fanning, so the paths keep a constant width. Each single average path also settles down on its own (this is the strong law of large numbers, a statement about whole paths); section 4 measures the weaker statement the assignment asks about, convergence in probability at each fixed n.

Law of large numbers: Sₙ/n converges in probability

For every tolerance ε > 0, the probability that the average misses the mean by more than ε goes to zero as n grows. I estimate that probability from the attackers and compare it with the exact value and two textbook bounds.

P( |Sn/n − μ| > ε ) → 0  as n → ∞,  for every ε > 0

The estimate is simple: among the m attackers, count those whose average is more than ε away from μ. The share has a 95% Wilson confidence interval. The exact probability comes from summing binomial probabilities. Two bounds hold for every n without any approximation:

Chebyshev's inequality uses only the variance: P ≤ σ²/(nε²). This is the one-line proof of the weak law of large numbers.
Hoeffding's inequality uses the fact that each step lies in an interval of length c (1 for Bernoulli, 2 for Rademacher): P ≤ 2 exp(−2nε²/c²). It falls exponentially fast instead of like 1/n.

The sample variance of each quantity across the m attackers, for every n, on log-log axes. A straight line of slope s means the variance grows like nˢ.

Var(Sn / na) = σ² n1 − 2a   so   a = 0: slope 1,  a = 1: slope −1,  a = ½: slope 0

All three normalizations divide Sₙ by a power of n, and dividing a random variable by c divides its variance by c². So the variance of Sₙ/nᵃ is σ² n1−2a. The exponent a = ½ is the only one that keeps the spread fixed, and that is why the central limit theorem is stated for Sₙ/√n. The slider adds a fourth line for any exponent you choose.

A caveat for the Bernoulli model. Sₙ/√n has a constant variance, but its mean √n p keeps growing, so it does not settle anywhere. To get a limit you must centre it first: (Sₙ − np)/√n has mean 0 and variance p(1 − p). In the Rademacher model μ = 0, so no centring is needed. This is why the assignment introduces the second model for the central limit theorem.

The Berry–Esseen theorem makes the central limit theorem quantitative: Dₙ ≤ C ρ / (σ³ √n) with ρ = E|X − μ|³ and C ≤ 0.4748 (Shevtsova, 2011). For Rademacher steps ρ = σ = 1, so the bound is 0.4748/√n. The exact distance turns out to be about 0.399/√n = 1/√(2πn), so the bound is only about 19% too high. The reason is the grid: Sₙ/√n can only take values 2/√n apart, so its distribution function jumps, and the largest jump (at 0) is about 2φ(0)/√n. A smooth curve can get no closer than half of that jump.

The gap and the test, n by n

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test asks whether the m simulated values could come from N(0, 1). At the 0.1% level it rejects for small n, where the gap is real and large, and stops rejecting once the true gap is smaller than what m attackers can detect. Not rejecting does not mean the distribution is exactly normal: it never is, since it lives on a grid.

What it means for n systems and m attackers

How the size of the system and the number of attackers change the aggregate penetration score, and where the i.i.d. model stops being realistic.

More systemsMore attackersSystems hit by anyoneAttackers of different skillLimits of the model

More systems: a bigger total, a predictable rate

For one attacker, a larger system means more breaches in total, np on average, and a larger absolute uncertainty, √(np(1 − p)). So the defender can say less and less about the exact number of breached systems. But the relative uncertainty, the spread of the breach rate Sₙ/n, shrinks like 1/√n. In a large network the share of systems an attacker gets into is almost exactly p. That is the law of large numbers as a planning tool: the rate can be predicted, the count cannot.

In the Rademacher model the expected net progress is zero for every n, yet the typical attacker does not stay near zero: it ends about √n steps away, ahead or behind. Half the attackers finish ahead, and with n = 10,000 systems a typical distance from the start is about 80 steps (the mean absolute value of a normal with standard deviation 100). Zero mean does not mean zero risk.

More attackers: more total damage, same individual picture

The attackers are independent copies of each other, so adding attackers does not change the distribution of any one attacker's score. What changes is the aggregate. The total number of breaches by all attackers, T = Sn(1) + … + Sn(m), is a sum of nm independent steps, so its mean nmp grows with both and its relative spread shrinks like 1/√(nm). Attackers of different skill

The i.i.d. model assumes every attacker has the same p. Suppose instead that attacker j has its own breach probability pⱼ, spread uniformly around p. Each attacker's average still converges, but to its own pⱼ, not to the common p. So across attackers, the variance of Sₙ/n no longer goes to zero: it stops at the variance of the skills, Var(pⱼ).

Limits of the model

AssumptionRealistic?What breaks if it fails
Independent systemsRarely. A stolen password or a shared vulnerability opens many systems at once.With a correlation ρ between systems, Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n + (1 − 1/n)ρσ², which tends to ρσ², not 0. The law of large numbers fails, and averaging over more systems stops helping.
Identical systemsNo. Systems differ in how hard they are.Mild: if system i has its own pᵢ, the average converges to the mean of the pᵢ, and the central limit theorem still holds (Lindeberg's condition), as long as no single system dominates.
Identical attackersNo. Skill varies a lot.Each attacker's average converges to its own skill; the spread across attackers never vanishes (chart above).
Independent attackersPartly. Attackers share tools and exploits.The total over attackers spreads more than √(nm), and the share of systems hit by anyone is lower than 1 − (1 − p)ᵐ, because attackers repeat each other's successes.
No learningNo. Attackers improve as they go deeper.The steps are no longer identically distributed in time; the average converges to the long-run rate, not to the first systems' rate.

Bonus: the most successful attacker

A defender worries about the best attacker, not the average one. Among m independent attackers, how far ahead is the best?

By the central limit theorem each attacker's standardized score Zⱼ = (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) is close to N(0, 1). The best of m is the maximum of m independent normals, which grows without limit but very slowly: roughly like √(2 ln m). Its exact expected value is E[max] = ∫ x · m φ(x) Φ(x)m−1 dx, which I compute numerically. To measure it, I split the simulated attackers at the largest n into groups of size g, take the best in each group, and average over the groups.

So the attacker pool size matters in a way that averages hide. Doubling the number of attackers leaves the average attacker unchanged but moves the best one further ahead. With a fixed alarm threshold set at a few standard deviations above the typical score, the chance that at least one attacker crosses it is 1 − Φ(t)ᵐ, which grows quickly with m: a threshold that one attacker crosses with probability 0.1% is crossed by at least one of 1,000 attackers with probability about 63%.
